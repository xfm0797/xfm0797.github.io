# The Scent of Camellia Seeds on the Mountain Path
After Frost's Descent, the wind in the mountains carried an extra chill. I was still lingering under the covers early in the morning when I heard the crisp clatter of bamboo baskets in the courtyard, followed by Grandma's voice: "Get up quick — it's a fine sunny day, time to pick the muzi."

Muzi is the camellia seed, the "autumn-winter treasure" of our village. The camellia grove behind the mountain was planted decades ago; its trunks are so thick it takes two people to wrap their arms around them, and among the deep green leaves hang clusters of greenish-brown fruit — some shells stretched tight, gleaming with an oily sheen; others have cracked open, revealing blackish-red seeds like babies unable to resist peeking out. Grandma always says the muzi picked after Frost's Descent is the plumpest, and only then does the pressed camellia oil have the finest aroma. So every year around this time, the whole family heads up the mountain to "race" the season.

I slipped on Mom's old rubber shoes, picked up a small bamboo basket, and followed behind. The mountain path was carpeted with withered yellow cogongrass that rustled underfoot; now and then a few white wild chrysanthemums poked out, dewy with morning mist, soaking my trouser legs. Grandma walked in front, the hem of her blue padded jacket fluttering in the wind, gripping a bamboo pole with an iron hook tied to the tip — her "old companion" of twenty years, made especially for hooking the muzi high up in the trees. "Walk slow," she called back, pointing her thin, gnarled finger at the roadside. "That patch of wild raspberries has ripened — we'll pick them after the seeds are done."

At the camellia grove, Mom had already found a short tree and stood on tiptoe picking the fruit within reach. With a crisp "snap," a camellia seed dropped into the bamboo basket cradled in her arm, the fuzz on its shell still clinging to fragments of leaves. "Just pick up the ones on the ground," she said with a smile, handing me a cloth. "Don't touch the thorns on the branches — you'll cut your hand." I crouched down and picked up the camellia seeds scattered in the withered grass one by one. My fingertips touched the shells — rock hard, carrying the coolness of the mountain. Some fruit had fallen into cracks between the rocks and had to be dug out with fingers; the creases of my nails were soon packed with dark brown soil, yet it didn't feel dirty at all — it was the mountain's mark, carrying the fresh, bitter fragrance of the camellia seeds.

Grandma was busy up above. Her iron hook caught a branch and gave it a gentle pull — with a "clatter," several camellia seeds came tumbling down. I rushed to catch them, but one smacked the back of my hand. It didn't hurt; it felt lively instead. "Girl, look at that tree!" Grandma pointed not far away. "Last year I picked a 'King of Muzi' there — bigger than my fist." I ran over to look, and sure enough there was a shallow notch on the trunk, a mark Grandma had made. Among the branches hung a round, plump fruit. I stood on tiptoe but couldn't reach it, spinning around in frustration, until Grandma came over laughing, plucked it in one motion, and handed it to me: "There — this year's 'King' belongs to you."

By the time the sun climbed overhead, the bamboo baskets were all full. We sat on the rocks in the camellia grove to rest. Mom pulled sweet potatoes and corn cakes out of her cloth bag, along with a thermos — the hot tea she poured rose in white wisps. The wind passed through the woods, carrying the light fragrance of camellia seeds mixed with the sweetness of sweet potatoes, so warm it made the heart flutter. Nibbling her corn cake, Grandma said picking muzi used to be even harder — they'd head up the mountain before dawn, carrying baskets taller than a person, walking dozens of li to the oil press. "Tea oil was precious back then; only during New Year could we bear to use it to stir-fry meat. Things are good now — oil pressed at home, eaten every day."

In the afternoon we headed down the mountain, everyone with a heavy bamboo basket on their back, bending their waists slightly. The setting sun stretched our shadows long, and the mountain path was filled with our footsteps and laughter. Back home, Mom poured the camellia seeds onto a bamboo mat in the courtyard, spreading them in a thin layer. Grandma took a bamboo rake and turned them over, one stroke at a time. "They must dry for three full days — only when the shells are dry are the seeds easy to shell." I crouched beside her, helping to break open the cracked ones and take out the seeds — blackish-red seeds dotted with shell fragments, heavy in the palm of my hand.

At dinner, Mom stir-fried greens with last year's camellia oil; the oil sizzled in the wok and the aroma filled the whole house. Sipping her rice wine, Grandma said once the seeds had dried and been shelled, she'd take them to the oil press. "This year's seeds are plump — the oil will surely be fragrant. I'll fill a jug for you to take back to the city." I took a bite of the greens — crisp and tender, with the mellow richness of camellia oil. Suddenly I understood: picking these mountain seeds, what we pick is fruit, what we dry is time, and what we press out is the everyday warmth of a family — simple, yet so grounded it warms the heart.

Lying in bed at night, I could still smell the camellia seed fragrance drifting from the courtyard, mingling with the evening breeze, gently settling on my pillow. Thinking of the day in the mountains — Grandma's iron hook, Mom's bamboo basket, the "King of Muzi" in my hand, and the camellia grove covering the slopes — I suddenly understood: the days of country folk are like these muzi. You must wait patiently for them to ripen, and pick, dry, and shell them with your own hands before you can taste their truest fragrance. And these everyday moments hidden along the mountain paths are the most precious times of all.
