# Mid-Autumn Night Talk
The evening breeze finally carried a hint of coolness, gently sweeping away the last trace of late-summer heat, when I suddenly noticed the osmanthus blooming on the branch by my desk. Tiny pale-yellow petals dotted the twigs, quietly kneading their sweet fragrance into the air — so much like the Mid-Autumn Festival itself: never a boisterous commotion, yet always, in the gentlest way, drawing the longing for reunion into everyone's heart.

When I was little, I always looked forward to Mid-Autumn — not so much for moon-watching, but for the blue-and-white porcelain plate in Grandma's hands. On it lay freshly baked mooncakes: the five-nut filling was the most substantial, and biting into it you could chew the full peanuts and rock sugar; the lotus paste ones wrapped salted egg yolks, their rich, oily fragrance floating across the whole little courtyard. Grandma would cut the mooncakes into small pieces and hand them to us as we sat gathered on bamboo chairs, while she herself held only a tiny piece of peach shortbread, smiling as she watched us scramble and chatter about school. The moon back then seemed especially round, hanging on the old locust tree at the corner of the courtyard, stretching the tree shadows long. We chased the shadows, our laughter landing on the grey brick floor, mingling with the rustling of Grandma's palm-leaf fan — the softest backdrop of my memories.

Later I left home for school, and Mid-Autumn was mostly spent far away. One year it fell on the busiest day of my class schedule; walking across campus after evening study, the streetlights dim and few passersby about. Suddenly I received a video call from Mom — on screen was the dining table at home, laid out with my favorite braised pork. Dad held up the phone and turned it around, saying, "Look, the moon is just as round as when you're home." I looked up at the sky, and there it was — a bright moon suspended in the ink-blue night, its clear light spilling across the glass of the teaching building, overlapping somehow with the moonlight of that little courtyard in my memory. In that moment I suddenly understood: Mid-Autumn was never about sitting around one table. It was that no matter how far apart, there is always a beam of moonlight that can tie our hearts together — just like how Mom always picks up an extra piece of meat on camera, saying "I ate it for you"; just like how I keep reminding them over the phone not to skimp on that newly bought box of mooncakes.

This Mid-Autumn I finally made it home. Pushing open the courtyard gate, I was surprised to see Grandma still sitting in the bamboo chair under the old locust tree, holding that same blue-and-white porcelain plate — except now it held several new flavors of mooncakes. Dad was busy in the kitchen, his humming of an old tune mixed with the whir of the range hood; Mom was collecting laundry on the balcony, and seeing me, she waved with a smile: "Come in quick — the lotus seed soup is still warm." I put down my luggage and walked over to help her fold the clothes. Out of the corner of my eye I caught the moon outside the window, quietly peeking out from behind the clouds, spilling its silver light over us.

It turns out that after growing up, the meaning of Mid-Autumn has quietly changed. No longer fixated on mooncake flavors, no longer chasing shadows, I instead treasure more the time of sitting together as a family — listening to Dad tell funny stories from work, listening to Mom gossip about the neighbors, listening to Grandma recall my childhood embarrassments, while now and then the scent of osmanthus drifts in through the window and settles into the white porcelain bowls brimming with lotus seed soup. The moonlight flows quietly, wrapping the laughter in the room in gentleness. In this moment I suddenly understand: what makes Mid-Autumn precious was never that round moon, but the people keeping watch beneath its light — that no matter how far you go, there is always a lamp, a table of food, a group of people waiting for you to come home.

Late at night, my family and I sat in the courtyard admiring the moon. Grandma handed me a five-nut mooncake — still the taste of childhood. Looking up at the moon, it suddenly seemed rounder and brighter than ever before. The breeze blew softly, the locust leaves rustled, the osmanthus fragrance washed over me — and for a moment I was a child again. Only this time, I am no longer the kid chasing shadows, but someone who has learned to hold tight the hands of those beside me, keeping this warmth of reunion safe in my heart.

- Written by XFM on the night of Mid-Autumn Festival, 2025
